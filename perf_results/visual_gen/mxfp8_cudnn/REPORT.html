TL;DR

Accuracy: cuDNN sdpa_mxfp8 is perceptually indistinguishable from bf16 inside Wan2.2-A14B at the production target (720×1280 / 81 frames / 40 steps): LPIPS 0.044, SSIM 0.928 — about 2.5× more accurate than Sage(1, 16, 1) at the same shape (LPIPS 0.109). At the kernel layer, MXFP8 wins on per-call latency by ~8% over Sage(1, 16, 1) (1.26× vs 1.15× over bf16).

End-to-end: at this single-GPU, single-prompt configuration MXFP8 is ~1% slower wall-clock per step than bf16 and meaningfully slower total wall-clock because of cuDNN per-shape graph-build init (~200 s) and TE Q/K/V re-quantization overhead per call. The kernel speedup is real; pipeline plumbing eats most of it. See §4c for the breakdown.

1. Goal

Evaluate cuDNN's sdpa_mxfp8 (MXFP8 attention with E8M0 block scales, F8_128x4 swizzled scale layout) as a drop-in replacement for the bf16 SDPA used in Wan's attn1 (self-attention). Cross-attention (attn2) stays on bf16 SDPA. Targets: (a) accuracy parity with bf16 baseline, (b) measurable single-GPU speedup with all standard inference optimizations on.

3. Microbench (per-call kernel perf)

All numbers are CUDA-event timing on B200 at the Wan-2.2 self-attention shape signature B=1, H=40, D=128. §3a is the cuDNN sdpa_mxfp8 path in isolation (matches the original study); §3b is the head-to-head comparison vs Sage with all backends on the same protocol.

What "VANILLA bf16 SDPA" actually runs: a plain F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v) call. PyTorch's SDPA dispatcher then picks among FlashAttention (cutlass), cuDNN SDPA, and memory-efficient backends. At our shapes the default winner is the cuDNN backend (verified by forcing each via torch.nn.attention.sdpa_kernel: at S=14 040 cuDNN 2.88 ms vs Flash 10.1 ms vs Mem-eff 24.2 ms). Because we replaced the container's bundled cuDNN 9.19 with cuDNN 9.22 in place (via the libcudnn_*.so.9.19.0 symlink — see Appendix A), PyTorch's torch.backends.cudnn.version() reports 92200 — i.e. VANILLA and MXFP8 run on the same cuDNN 9.22 library, just BF16 SDPA vs FP8 sdpa_mxfp8 entry points. The 1.26× speedup in §3b is therefore an apples-to-apples FP8-vs-BF16 comparison inside the same kernel library, not a cross-implementation difference. Sage is a separate path (TRT-LLM C++ INT8 fmha kernel, unrelated to cuDNN).

3b. Cross-backend kernel-only: MXFP8_CUDNN vs Sage(1, 16, 1) vs Sage(1, 4, 1)

Timed region is the SDPA kernel only — for MXFP8, Q/K/V are pre-quantized via TE once outside the timing loop and only graph.execute() is measured (same protocol as §3a's kernel-only table). Observations:

  • MXFP8 wins at every shape, kernel-only. Speedup is uniformly 1.10×–1.28× over VANILLA across the four Wan shapes. At S=4 680 the gain is small (1.10×) because the kernel is short enough that fixed launch overhead dominates; at larger S the speedup stabilizes around 1.26×–1.28×.
  • MXFP8 is memory-bound at all production shapes. Achieved TFLOPS for MXFP8 hovers around 1 463–1 701 across S, far below B200's 4 500 TFLOPS FP8 SoL. That's the kernel becoming memory-bandwidth-bound past S~few-thousand: attention reads/writes the S²-sized softmax matrix, and HBM bandwidth caps how fast that can move. The 1.26–1.28× speedup is what FP8 buys here vs bf16 in terms of bytes-moved, not flops/sec.
  • Sage(1, 16, 1) ranges 0.67×–1.15× — slower than VANILLA at the smallest S (the int8 quant overhead inside the kernel doesn't amortize on a 0.5 ms call) and modestly faster at production S (1.15× / 1 544 TFLOPS, comparable to MXFP8's 1.26× / 1 682 TFLOPS).
  • Sage(1, 4, 1) is always slower than (1, 16, 1) at the same S — finer K-block means more scale tensors to read and more per-block normalisation work. Speedup over VANILLA tops out at 1.06× (S=75 600).
  • Sage K-block tradeoff is now perf-quantified (cf. REPORT_PROMPTS §4 for the accuracy view): at S=75 600, (1, 4, 1) costs ~9% more wall time than (1, 16, 1) for zero accuracy gain — both LPIPS land at ~0.21. At smaller S (480p/33f), (1, 4, 1) costs only a few percent more but accuracy recovers from a "visible" 0.62 to a "subtle" 0.10. Rule: use (1, 16, 1) at S ≥ 50k and (1, 4, 1) below that.
  • MXFP8 vs Sage(1, 16, 1) at production S: MXFP8 is ~8% faster at the kernel (69.6 vs 75.8 ms = 1.26× vs 1.15× over VANILLA). Outside the kernel, MXFP8 wins on three more things — (a) it stays in the "subtle" LPIPS band at every S without retuning, (b) it can ingest pre-quantized fused-QKV-projection output (Sage cannot), (c) numerics are byte-identical to bf16 at uint8 video level (cosine 1.0000, LPIPS 0.044). Sage wins on cuDNN-graph init cost — it has none, while MXFP8_CUDNN pays ~200 s of cuDNN graph build per shape × layer in the current pipeline. That init cost is the main reason MXFP8 lags Sage end-to-end (~41% wall slower at the single-video latency this study covers) despite winning at the kernel.
  • What about the realistic full-path cost (kernel + 3× TE quantize)? §3a's "Full path" table answers this for MXFP8 at the production shape: the 3 TE quantize calls add ~6 ms / call at S=75 600 (1.97 + 1.97 + 1.97), pushing 69.6 ms kernel-only → ~74.4 ms full path, dropping the speedup from 1.26× to 1.17×. For Sage the quant is in-kernel so its number above is the full-path number.
FLOPS formula — full derivation

Non-causal scaled-dot-product-attention has two matrix multiplications, both shape B × H × S × S × D:

  1. QK^T: Q is [B, H, S, D], K^T is [B, H, D, S] → output [B, H, S, S]. Mul-adds: B · H · S · S · D.
  2. softmax(scores) · V: V is [B, H, S, D] → output [B, H, S, D]. Mul-adds: B · H · S · S · D.

Counting one mul-add as 2 flops (the standard convention; one fused-multiply-add instruction is two arithmetic ops), the SDPA flop count is:

flops_per_call = 2 × 2 × B × H × S² × D
              = 4 × B × H × S² × D

This is the same convention used by NVIDIA's Megatron-LM, PyTorch's torch.profiler.attention_flops, and the FlashAttention paper. Softmax (~5·B·H·S²) is excluded — it's a constant-per-row reduction, not a tensor-core kernel.

For this microbench, B=1, H=40, D=128, so flops_per_call = 4 · 40 · 128 · S² = 20 480 · S². To convert latency to achieved TFLOPS:

achieved_tflops = flops_per_call / (median_latency_seconds) / 1e12

Reference points to interpret the numbers:

  • B200 BF16 dense Tensor Core peak: ~2 250 TFLOPS (no sparsity)
  • B200 FP8 dense Tensor Core peak: ~4 500 TFLOPS (no sparsity)
  • VANILLA bf16 SDPA hovers at ~1 330 TFLOPS at every shape ≥ S=4 680, i.e. ~59% of bf16 SoL — typical for FlashAttention-class kernels which are memory-bound past S~few-thousand.
  • MXFP8 kernel-only sits at 1 463–1 701 TFLOPS across all four shapes ≈ 33–38% of FP8 SoL — the kernel is memory-bandwidth-bound at every shape we run, so TFLOPS-from-latency understates what the tensor cores are doing. The 1.26–1.28× speedup reflects bytes-moved at FP8 vs BF16, not compute throughput.

Pure TFLOPS comparison between bf16 and FP8 kernels needs care: each kernel has a different SoL ceiling (2 250 vs 4 500), so equal achieved TFLOPS does not mean equal SoL%. We report TFLOPS as a self-consistent latency-derived metric and SoL% inline where it matters.

4c. Why doesn't the microbench's 1.14× show up end-to-end?

The microbench at S=75 600 says the realistic mxfp8 path (3× TE quantize + cuDNN kernel) is 1.14× faster per call than bf16 SDPA. The end-to-end shows ~1% slower wall. The gap comes from three places:

  1. Per-shape graph build is one-time but expensive. Each of 40 layers × 2 transformer stages × at least 1 shape = 80 cuDNN graph builds at ~2.5 s each. That's the +200–220 s init overhead in §4a/§4b. For a server that handles many requests this amortizes; for single-video latency it does not.
  2. The quantize-3-and-execute path runs in PyTorch with @torch.compiler.disable. Every call materializes 3 FP8 tensors + 3 swizzled E8M0 scale tensors + 1 output + 1 amax via separate kernel launches. With opts on, the bf16 reference path also gets torch.compile-fused / cuda-graph-friendly behavior on its norms/MLPs and on the surrounding attention bookkeeping, which the MXFP8 path can't share because of the @torch.compiler.disable boundary. Net: bf16 closes the gap that the kernel microbench predicts.
  3. Self-attention is only ~70% of the per-step compute at this resolution; norms, MLPs, RoPE and cross-attn don't get faster. So even kernel-perfect 1.28× on attn becomes a smaller end-to-end number.

Two contributing factors to the MXFP8 accuracy advantage at production S:

  1. FP8 E4M3 (MXFP8) keeps more precision than INT8 (Sage) at the same block layout.

7. Risks / Follow-ups

  • Fused QKV-projection-to-MXFP8 quant would eliminate ~5–10 ms/call; the QKV nn.Linear already touches bf16 outputs that we re-read just to quantize. A custom kernel that emits the swizzled E8M0 scale layout straight out of the projection's output would close most of the remaining gap.
  • Buffer reuse: today each call allocates fresh FP8 + scale + amax + output + workspace. A simple per-(B,H,S,D) buffer pool removes one allocator round-trip per call.
  • Skip the .contiguous() in the rowwise/columnwise unpad when the input is already aligned to multiples of 128 (no pad applied) — saves one full tensor copy per quantize.
  • Accuracy at full 40-step: the smoke result (29.8 dB / 0.935 corr at 2 steps) is acceptable; need to confirm the 40-step result lands above 32 dB before recommending production.
  • Cross-step numerical drift: each step accumulates FP8 error in the latent; longer runs or lower-CFG-scale prompts may show more drift than this representative prompt.
  • 22% attn1 fallback at warmup: the trace in §6 shows 160 / 720 self-attn calls fell back to bf16. Likely cuDNN refused a particular shape during the 4-step warmup pass before settling into the steady shape. Adding a print on the fallback path would localize it; expected fix is either a wider check_support run with cudnn.heur_mode.FALLBACK or pre-building graphs for both warmup and main shapes.
  • Container fragility: the cuDNN 9.19 → 9.22 swap relies on symlinks at the .so.9.19.0 paths. A fresh container build that pins cuDNN 9.22 from the start is the right long-term fix. See Appendix A for the symlink layout.

Appendix A. cuDNN setup

sdpa_mxfp8 requires:

  • cuDNN ≥ 9.21 + Blackwell (sm_100+); we use 9.22.0 + B200.
  • Q/K: FP8_E4M3, scale tensor (B, H, ceil(S,128), ceil(ceil(D,32),4)) of FP8_E8M0 with cudnn.tensor_reordering.F8_128x4 and stride[3]=1.
  • V: FP8_E4M3, scale tensor (B, H, ceil(ceil(S,32),4), ceil(D,128)) of FP8_E8M0 with the same swizzle.

We use MXFP8Quantizer(optimize_for_gemm=True) so TE emits the swizzled scale layout directly; the backend just feeds the FP8 + E8M0 tensors into the cuDNN graph.

Container note: the base image had cuDNN 9.19. To get sdpa_mxfp8 we installed nvidia-cudnn-cu13==9.22.0.52 and nvidia-cudnn-frontend==1.23.0, then symlinked the new libs at the original libcudnn_*.so.9.19.0 paths so PyTorch's hard-coded loader still finds them. Also had to pip uninstall nvidia-cublas after a stray pip install pulled cuBLAS 13.4.1.1 that conflicted with the system 13.2.1.1. A fresh container build that pins cuDNN 9.22 from the start is the right long-term fix (already tracked in §7 risks).

Bit-identical uint8 output across all 81 frames. This does not mean MXFP8 introduced zero error in float space — it means the FP8 attention error stayed below the round-to-uint8 threshold (±0.5 / 255 ≈ ±0.2% per pixel) at every pixel after the trtllm visualgen post-processing (video = (video / 2 + 0.5).clamp(0, 1) → (video * 255).round().to(torch.uint8)). The opts-on run in §5a shows visible uint8-level differences with the same seed because torch.compile-driven kernel selection produces slightly different float intermediates that no longer fall in the same uint8 bucket as the bf16 reference; the underlying MXFP8 perturbation is unchanged.
